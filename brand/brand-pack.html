01 — Logo

The lockup

Two versions cover almost everything. Use the full-colour lockup on light backgrounds and the reversed lockup on navy or on photography. Never retype the wordmark; always use the supplied files.

Full colour — on paper white or any light, low-detail background.

Reversed — on navy, or over a darkened photograph.

Clear space

Keep clear space around the lockup equal to the height of the coin mark on all four sides. Nothing (type, edges, other logos) comes inside it.

Minimum size

Do not reproduce the lockup below 32 mm wide in print, or 120 px wide on screen. Below that the strapline stops being legible; use the coin mark alone instead.

The coin mark

The £ coin works on its own where the full lockup won't fit: social avatars, favicons, app icons, stamps on photography.

Don't

  • Recolour it. The grey and orange are fixed; the only alternative is the all-white reversed version.
  • Stretch, squash, rotate or add effects (shadows, outlines, gradients).
  • Place the full-colour lockup on a busy photograph or a mid-tone background.
  • Rebuild the wordmark in another typeface.

02 — Colour

The one orange rule

This is the single most common way to get the brand wrong, so it's worth reading twice. Penny orange is not a text colour on white. It measures 2.3:1 against white, which fails accessibility standards at every size. It looks fine on a big screen in a bright room and becomes unreadable everywhere else.

CombinationRatioVerdictUse for
#F7931E on #FFFFFF2.3:1FailsNever for text. Shapes and rules only.
#A64D07 on #FCFBF85.1:1Passes AAOrange text, links and small headings on light.
#16233C on #F7931E6.7:1Passes AAButtons: dark text on an orange fill.
#FFFFFF on #F7931E2.3:1FailsNever. White on orange is unreadable.
#F7931E on #0B1B336.7:1Passes AAOrange text and accents on navy.
#0E2038 on #FCFBF815.3:1Passes AAABody copy and headings.

Rule of thumb for social graphics: if orange is touching a letterform on a light background, change it to #A64D07. If orange is a block, a rule or a shape, leave it as #F7931E.

03 — Typography

Two typefaces

Both are free for commercial use from Fontshare (Indian Type Foundry) and can be downloaded and installed on your machine for use in Canva, Figma, Word or anywhere else.

Aa

Cabinet Grotesk

Headlines and anything big. Weights 700 (Bold) and 800 (Extrabold) only. Never use it for body copy.

fontshare.com/fonts/cabinet-grotesk

Aa

Switzer

Body copy, labels, buttons, captions. Weights 400 (Regular) and 600 (Semibold) only.

fontshare.com/fonts/switzer

Hierarchy comes from size, not weight

The brand uses only two weights per face. Don't reach for a heavier weight to make something stand out; make it bigger, or give it more space around it. Large display type is tracked slightly tight; body copy is not.

04 — Layout & UI

Ruled, not boxed

The layout language is a ledger: thin hairlines organising content, plenty of white space, and square corners. Avoid drop shadows, rounded cards and glows — they pull the brand towards generic template territory.

Corners

  • Panels, images and dividers: square, 0 radius.
  • Buttons: fully rounded pill (999px).
  • Form fields: square, 1px hairline border.

Spacing rhythm

Three tiers, never one uniform gap. Tight for closely related blocks, standard between sections, and a statement gap around anything that should land on its own.

Buttons

Make an enquiry See finance options

Primary: penny fill, ink text. Secondary: hairline outline, fills on hover. Generous horizontal padding — never let text crowd the edge.

Applying this to a social post

  • Navy background, reversed logo top-left, one big Cabinet Grotesk line, plenty of empty space.
  • One short penny-orange rule above the headline as the only decoration.
  • If you put type over a photo, darken the photo with a navy overlay first so the text stays readable.
  • Don't fill the frame. If it feels a bit empty, it's probably right.
  • Don't add a second accent colour, gradients, or emoji.

05 — Photography

Show what the money buys

Pictures earn their place by showing the thing being financed: a van, a machine, racking full of stock, premises, a shop counter. Real British working businesses in good daylight. Avoid anything that could be anywhere.

Assets: vans, plant, machinery.

Premises and high streets.

Stock, racking, operations.

Look for

  • Bright, natural daylight and plenty of room around the subject
  • UK-plausible detail: British plates, brick, hedgerows, high streets
  • Real work in progress rather than posed models
  • Clean, uncluttered compositions

Reject

  • Any foreign signage or language in shot, however small
  • Left-hand-drive vehicles and non-UK number plates
  • Dark, oily, cluttered interiors
  • Handshakes, suits round a laptop, boardrooms
  • Coins, piggy banks, calculators, stock charts

Always open a picture at full size before you use it. Thumbnails hide the giveaways. Several otherwise-good shots were rejected for the site only once enlarged, revealing foreign-language signage or an American road sign in the background.

06 — Tone of voice

Say it like you'd say it

Plain UK English, short sentences, first person plural. The reader is a business owner with a job to do. Write the way you'd talk to them across a yard, not the way a bank writes a letter.

Do

  • Be concrete: "a van, a machine, a cash-flow gap"
  • Vary sentence length. Short ones land.
  • Say what something costs and what happens next
  • Admit limits: "if there isn't a sensible option, we'll say so"

Don't

  • "Unlock", "seamless", "elevate", "empower", "bespoke solutions"
  • "In today's fast-paced business landscape…"
  • "It's not just finance, it's a partnership"
  • Exclamation marks, emoji, or three adjectives in a row
  • Promising rates, speed or approval you can't guarantee

Before anything goes out

Anything that promotes finance is a financial promotion and needs sign-off before publication, including social posts and outbound email. Don't quote rates, don't promise timescales, and don't imply a decision is guaranteed. When in doubt, describe what you do and invite a conversation.
